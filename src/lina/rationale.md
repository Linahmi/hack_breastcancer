# Why model surprise is not enough

I scored every possible single amino-acid substitution in the trastuzumab scFv with two protein language models. ESM2 650M is a general protein model; AbLang2 is trained specifically on antibody repertoires. At each position, I masked the parent residue and compared the model probability of each substitution with that of the wild-type residue. Neither model has seen HER2, and neither uses the antibody–antigen structure. Their scores measure how statistically plausible a residue is in its sequence context. That can be useful for identifying changes compatible with folding and stability, but it is not a prediction of binding affinity.

A common way to use these scans is to retain only positions where the model ranks at least one non-wild-type residue above the parent residue. The interpretation is that the existing residue looks wrong to the model and may therefore be an opportunity. For this molecule, that interpretation is unsafe. Trastuzumab is not a natural antibody sampled from a repertoire: murine binding loops were grafted onto a human framework and then altered during laboratory affinity maturation. The human framework consequently looks familiar to both models, while residues selected for binding can look unusual precisely because they were engineered.

The filter therefore risks firing on residues that are atypical for a good reason. A high score for a replacement at such a site does not show that the replacement will improve the antibody; it may simply show that it would make the sequence look more natural while undoing part of the binding solution. AbLang2 adds a second, related bias. Because it was trained on natural antibody repertoires, it tends to prefer germline-like residues. The generic “replace surprising residues” rule and the antibody-specific pull toward germline can thus nominate the same positions through different mechanisms. Their agreement is not independent evidence for improved affinity.

We use the structural contact map to resolve this ambiguity. A surprising residue that contacts HER2 is plausibly an intentional part of the engineered interface and should be treated with suspicion, not automatically reverted. A surprising residue outside the interface is more plausibly a genuine sequence liability and is a better candidate for improving foldability or expression without directly rewriting the binding surface. This is a prioritisation rule, not a guarantee: non-contact mutations can still affect affinity indirectly, and contact mutations can sometimes improve it.

Accordingly, germline-reverting and non-germline-reverting designs should form separate arms of the 15-sequence submission. Keeping them separate makes the biological hypotheses visible instead of mixing two different mechanisms into one ranking. Our central hypothesis is that model surprise becomes useful only after structural context distinguishes likely engineering from likely liability. We cannot test that claim today: the models are target-blind, and the binding and expression measurements will not return for three weeks. Until then, this is a rationale for allocating experimental risk, not evidence of improved variants.
